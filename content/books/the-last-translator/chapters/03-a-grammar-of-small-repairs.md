# A Grammar of Small Repairs

Sefa’s grandmother had taught her the imperfect with a story, because that is how the imperfect is taught.

*“You say it like this,” she said, “when the thing is not finished. When the bread is rising. When the child is asleep and you are not sure he will stay asleep. When somebody is dead and you are still talking to them.”*

The Vesh imperfect had one form and eleven uses, and the Bureau’s dictionary had deleted all eleven and replaced them with a single note: *archaic, avoid.*

Sefa began to write her certification report, and then she stopped, and began to write a different document.

It took her four days. It had five sections and an appendix, and the appendix was a table of 118 sentences in Vesh, each with a literal translation, each with a note explaining what the imperfect was doing, each with a citation from the Bureau’s own record of the language before the dictionary.

The argument was simple, and it fit on one page:

A language cannot be certified dead if its grammar has been administratively amputated. The Bureau removed the imperfect in 2019. The imperfect is still in use in every market in the district. What the Bureau certified was not a death. It was an injury, and the injury was committed by the certifying body, and a certification that conceals the injury is not a certification.

She typed it. She printed it. She read it aloud in her kitchen at two in the morning, in Vesh, and then in the Bureau’s language, and the Bureau’s language made it sound like a complaint about paperwork, which is what the Bureau’s language was for.

Then she called the officer and said she would attend the hearing, and that she would bring the appendix, and that she wanted the Bureau’s own grammarian to attend.

There was a pause on the line.

“That is not the procedure,” the officer said.

“I know,” Sefa said. “I’m learning that phrase.”
