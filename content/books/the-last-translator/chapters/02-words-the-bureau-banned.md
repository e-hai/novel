# Words the Bureau Banned

There were two hundred and six words on the Bureau’s banned list, and Sefa had grown up using all of them.

They were not profanity and they were not slurs. They were words for things the Bureau had decided did not need names: the feeling of a house after a funeral, the specific grief of a mother who outlives a child, the way light falls in a room where someone has just left, the sound of a language being spoken by fewer people this year than last.

Vesh had a word for each of those. The Bureau had a rule for each of those words.

Sefa began the documentation on a Thursday, in the market, with a notebook and a recorder and the license in her coat pocket, which she had worn every day since the contract arrived.

She recorded forty-one conversations in six days. She wrote down 312 instances of banned words, used by fishwives and bus drivers and children and a man who repaired bicycles and did not know the word was banned, and who laughed when she told him and said, “Then what do I call it?”

She wrote that down too.

On the sixth night she opened the Bureau’s dictionary, the official one, the one that had replaced the schools, and compared it to the dictionary her grandmother had kept, which was a paperback with a cracked spine and 1,200 entries.

The official dictionary had 940.

Not 940 words missing. 940 words *total*, in a language that her grandmother’s book said had 1,200, and which the Bureau’s own preface said had “approximately 1,180 recorded forms.”

Sefa read the preface twice.

Then she read the grammar appendix, and found that the Bureau had removed the imperfect aspect, which is the tense you use in Vesh to describe something that was happening and is still, in some way, happening.

Without the imperfect, a language can report. It cannot mourn.
