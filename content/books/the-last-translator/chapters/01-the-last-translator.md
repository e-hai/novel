# The Last Translator

The Bureau hired Sefa Orel on a Monday, in a room with no windows and a table with two chairs, and told her the job in four sentences.

“The Vesh language is dead. Certification requires a licensed translator. You are the last one. The fee is twelve thousand.”

She had been expecting six.

“When?” she said.

“The hearing is in nine days.”

“And the certification is a document.”

“The certification is a document,” the officer said, “and the document says the language has no living speakers, no untranslated literature, and no continuing use in any domain. You sign, and the archive closes, and the language becomes a subject.”

Sefa put her hands flat on the table.

The Vesh language had nine thousand speakers when she was born. It had four thousand when she was licensed. It had nine hundred when the Bureau published the first dictionary, which was also when the schools stopped teaching it, which was also when the radio stopped broadcasting in it, which was also when the last Vesh-language newspaper printed its last issue with a headline that said, in Vesh, *We will print again on Thursday*.

“Do you speak it?” the officer asked.

“I am licensed.”

“That is not the question.”

“No,” Sefa said. “You are asking whether I can be trusted to say it is dead.”

The officer did not smile. “I am asking whether you can be trusted to say it accurately.”

She took the contract home in a paper envelope and put it on the kitchen table and did not sign it for two days.

On the third day she went to the market, to the stall where the old women sold fish and argued about the price of fish, and listened, for an hour, to a language that was supposed to have no continuing use in any domain.
