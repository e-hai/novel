# The Dictionary Burns Clean

The Bureau’s answer came in three days, in writing, on paper, which Sefa found almost polite.

The certification was refused. The archive would be closed and the physical holdings destroyed, as was standard for a language with no living speakers. The measure was scheduled for the last Friday of the month. The fee was withdrawn.

She read it standing at her kitchen window, and then she read it again, and then she did the one thing she had been preparing to do since the market.

She called her grandmother’s students.

There were nine of them. They were not linguists. They were a retired bus mechanic, two schoolteachers, a nurse, a printer, a woman who ran a laundry, a man who sold vegetables, a boy of nineteen who had learned from his mother, and a priest who had learned it to hear confessions.

They met in the laundry on a Tuesday night, and Sefa gave each of them a section of the dictionary, and a recording, and a list of the banned words, and the appendix, and a date.

“You have it in your mouth,” she said. “The Bureau can burn the paper. It cannot burn nine people.”

The printer printed three hundred copies of the appendix on the laundry’s machine, in the back room, at night, and the boy of nineteen delivered them, on a bicycle, to every address in the district where a Vesh speaker was known to live.

On the last Friday of the month the Bureau burned the archive in a yard behind the customs house, and it burned clean, exactly as the notice said it would: no smoke, no complaint, a fire that had been designed by someone who understood their work.

Sefa stood at the fence and watched.

Beside her, the priest said, in Vesh, quietly, with the imperfect, which is the tense you use when something is happening and is still happening: *They are burning it. I am still hearing it.*

The fire went on. The language did not.
