# What the Bell Remembers

The bell at Carrick Point had been cast in 1841 from the metal of a wreck. It was rung for fog, for fire, and for the dead, and it had rung more for the dead than for the other two.

All through April it rang on clear days.

Nell kept a chart of it. Nine strokes on the fourth. Two on the eleventh. One on the nineteenth, at eleven at night, so loud that the village sent a boy up the hill to ask whether the light was out. It was not out. The wick was trimmed, the oil was full, and the beam turned.

On the twenty-first, she opened the ledger to write the weather and found the page already written.

*21 April. Wind E 3. Bell, nine. Cutter Marren, of St. Ives, six hands. Saved.*

Her own hand. Her own ink. She had been asleep at the hour the entry named.

She read the line until the letters stopped being words. Then she went down to the harbour and asked about the *Marren*, and was told the cutter had come in that morning on the tide, six hands, with her mast sprung and her pumps going, and that she had passed the Point in the dark because the bell had rung and the skipper had heard it and turned.

Nell walked back up the hill with the wind in her coat and understood the trade at last, and hated it.

The book did not only take. Sometimes it gave. It wrote a wreck before the wreck and a rescue before the rescue, and it did not tell you which you were reading until the tide came in.

At the Point, she pulled the bell rope until her hands were raw, and then she sat on the step with the ledger open on her knees and wrote, in the margin, where no one had written before:

*Who is keeping this book?*
