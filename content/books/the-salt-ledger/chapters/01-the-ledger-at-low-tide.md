# The Ledger at Low Tide

The ledger came to Nell Rourke the way most things came to her family: wet, late, and unexplained.

Her father had kept the light at Carrick Point for thirty-one years. He died in March. The board sent a man to collect the lamp, the keys, and the book. The man found the book half under the cot, its covers swollen with salt, and he did not want it. “That’s keeper’s business,” he said, and left it on the kitchen table.

Nell opened it at the back, where the entries were recent.

*12 February. Wind SW 4. Light steady. No traffic.*
*19 February. Wind SW 6. Light steady. Ardent Goose, out of Falmouth, eleven hands, overdue.*
*3 March. Wind NW 3. Light steady. Ardent Goose, eleven hands. Lost off the Black Shoal. Bodies: four.*

Her father had written the loss in March. But the *Ardent Goose* had gone down on the eleventh of March, and the board’s inquiry had not closed until June. Nell had read the newspaper account twice, standing in the post office, because her father’s name was in it and because the number of bodies had changed between the first report and the second.

She turned back a page.

*2 March. Wind NW 3. Light steady. Ardent Goose, eleven hands. Lost off the Black Shoal.*

There it was, nine days early, in the same hand, in the same ink.

She read it again in the lamp room, with the tide out and the rocks showing their teeth. Below the point, the Black Shoal lay bare and black, and gulls stood on it like men waiting for a ferry.

Her father had written a wreck before it happened. Or someone had written it for him, which was worse, because Nell had spent her life believing that the worst thing a keeper could do was lie about the light.

She took the book home and set it by the stove to dry. Overnight the pages curled. In the morning, a new entry had appeared at the bottom of the last page, in a hand so like her father’s that she checked the kitchen for his coat.

*25 March. Wind E 2. Light steady. Nell Rourke, of the Point. Ledger open.*
