# The Keeper's Daughter

Her father’s letters were in a tin under the stairs. Nell had never read them; she had been busy being angry at him for dying without telling her how the lamp worked, as if the lamp were a secret and not a machine.

There were forty-one letters, all to a man named Coyle, who had kept the light at Bell Head before her father came. They were not friendly letters. They were instructions.

*The book must be written before the weather, not after. If you write after, the board asks how you knew. If you write before, the board asks who told you. Write before and let them ask.*

*Never write a name you have not seen on a manifest. The book will take a name from your mouth and put it in the water.*

*When they come to close it, do not let them. A closed book is a book nobody reads. An open book is a book somebody has to answer for.*

Nell read them twice in the kitchen, with the stove going and the wind coming up.

Her father had not been a liar. He had been a keeper of an open book, which was worse for everyone, and which was the family trade.

She went down to the lamp room and looked at the shelf where the book had lived. There was a ring of salt on the wood, and inside the ring a shape like a small hand.

Then she took the ledger up to the Point, where the bell hung in its iron frame, and set it on the step, and waited for the bell to ring, because her father’s letters said it would.

It rang at four, with no wind and no sea, one stroke, and the gulls went up all at once like a sheet lifted off a table.

Nell wrote the hour in the book.

Under it, in her own hand, she wrote the sentence she would spend the rest of the year defending.

*The book stays open.*
