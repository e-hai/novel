# The Harvest Ledger

The ledger was in the vestry, in a chest with two locks, and Mrs. Tull gave him the keys on his third Sunday without being asked.

“You’ll want to write in it,” she said. “Everyone does.”

It was not a parish register. Parish registers have baptisms in them, and marriages, and the dates of burials. This book had names, and after each name a number, and after each number a word.

*Tull, Martha. 7. Holding.*
*Ash, Peter. 12. Held.*
*Renn, Alice. 3. Green.*

The numbers were years. The words were the state of the tree.

Cade read to the end of the book, where the ink was fresh, and found his own name.

*Cade, Father. 0. Planted.*

He had not written it. He had been in Orra for twenty-two days. His hand was not in the book, and yet the letters had the small leftward lean that he had carried since school and could not lose.

He took the book to Ash and asked him to explain it, and Ash read the entry and did not look surprised, which was the worst thing he could have done.

“The orchard keeps its own book,” Ash said. “We only read it.”

“It has my name in it.”

“It has everyone’s name in it.” Ash turned the page. “Look. Here is mine. *Ash, Peter. 12. Held.*” He put a thumb on the entry. “I am sixty-eight. I was planted at twelve. That was the year my mother died, and there was no money for a tree, and the church paid.”

“So you are in the orchard.”

“I am in the orchard,” Ash said. “And when I die, I will be in the orchard in a way that the ground would not allow. That is not a curse, Father. That is a bill that somebody paid.”

Cade looked at the blank tags at the back of the row, and at the numbers after the names, and asked the question he had been avoiding.

“Who pays for the twelve at the back?”

Ash closed the book.
