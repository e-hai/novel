# What the Trees Are For

The rule was old enough that nobody could say when it started, which in Orra meant it had always been true.

Nothing was buried in the ground. The ground in Orra was thin and wet and given to returning what it was given. So the dead went into the orchard instead, and the orchard kept them, and in November the orchard showed what it had kept.

“Bones,” Cade said.

“Bones,” said Mr. Ash, who was the oldest and had the job of explaining things to priests. “Bones, and sometimes a little more.”

“How much more?”

“Depends on the person.”

They walked the rows together. Ash named the trees as they passed: *Tull, 1901. Tull, 1938. Ash, 1962. Ash, 1971.* The names were on small iron tags wired to the trunks, and the tags were rusted the way old tags are, and some of them were new.

Cade asked what happened if a family had no tree.

“Then they plant one,” Ash said. “It’s a cost. A tree takes seven years before it holds.”

“And if they can’t afford one?”

Ash looked at him the way Mrs. Tull had looked at him.

“Then they come to the church,” he said. “That’s why we have a church.”

That night Cade counted the tags by lamplight, walking the rows in the cold, and found something that made him stop: a row at the back where the tags were blank.

Twelve of them. Blank iron, wired to twelve trees, and under each tree the ground was swept clean, as if someone had been there recently and did not want the bones to be seen.

He wrote in his notebook: *12 unnamed. Ask Ash. Ask Tull. Do not ask the village.*
