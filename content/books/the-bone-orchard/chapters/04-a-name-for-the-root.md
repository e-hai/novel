# A Name for the Root

The blank tags were not blank because the people were unknown. They were blank because nobody had agreed to be written on them.

Ash told him on a walk, in the rain, in a way that made it clear the walk had been arranged.

“A tree holds a name,” he said. “That’s the whole trick of it. Not a body. A name. The body goes in and the tree takes the name and grows it. If there’s no name, the tree takes whatever is nearest, and whatever is nearest is not usually a person.”

“So the twelve at the back—”

“Are trees that took the wrong thing.”

Cade stopped walking. Rain ran off the brim of his hat.

“The twelve are children,” Ash said. “From the year the fever took the school. Nobody had money for trees and the church paid for twelve, and the church wrote twelve names down, and none of the families would sign. They said a name in a tree is a soul in a tree, and they would rather have nothing.”

“So the trees took the wrong thing.”

“They took the fever,” Ash said. “The fever is still in them. That’s why the ground is swept. That’s why the row is at the back.”

They stood in the rain and looked down at the village, which was small and grey and had one church and one orchard and forty-one people.

“What do you want from me?” Cade asked.

“You’re the priest,” Ash said. “You’re the one who can write a name and say it’s a name.”

That night Cade took the ledger and a pen and went out to the back row, and stood under the first blank tree, and wrote, in his own hand, the one name he could defend:

*Cade, Father. 0. Planted.*

He wired the tag to the trunk himself. The iron was cold and the wire cut his thumb.

Behind him, in the dark, something in the branches shifted with a sound like a drawer opening.
