# Ripe

They did not send him away. Orra was a village of forty-one people, and a priest who will not read a name a mother would not sign is a priest you keep, even if you keep him carefully.

The twelve families came to the church one at a time, over a week, and each of them brought a name.

Cade wrote them in the ledger in the order they came, and after each name he wrote *0. Planted*, which is what you write for a tree that has been waiting.

The last was Mrs. Tull, who brought a name and would not say it aloud, and handed him a slip of paper with a child’s writing on it.

He wrote it. He read it back to her. She said, “Yes,” and then she said, “Thank you,” and then she went out into the rain without her lamp.

That night the back row changed.

Cade saw it from the church path: twelve trees, twelve tags, and on the branches, where the bones had been, there was nothing at all, only clean white wood and the beginning of leaves, in November, in the cold, where nothing grows.

He went out with the lantern and stood under the first tree and put his hand on the trunk. It was warm.

Ash came up behind him and stood there without speaking for a while.

“They’re ripe,” he said at last.

“Ripe how?”

“Ripe as in done.” Ash took off his hat. “The fever’s gone out of them. That’s what a name does. It gives the tree something to hold that isn’t the wrong thing.”

Cade looked down the row at the twelve clean trees, and then at the far end of the orchard where his own tag hung on a young tree, and at the bones on all the other branches, which were still there, still white, still waiting for the year to be read out.

“Will they bury them now?” he asked.

“They’ll bury them in the spring,” Ash said. “That’s the rule. You don’t bury in November. November is for reading.”

He turned and went down the slope toward the village, and Cade stood in the orchard a while longer, with the lantern and the book and the twelve trees that had been given their names back.

Then he wrote the year’s last entry, in the vestry, by the lamp, in his own hand:

*The book stays open. The names are written. The fever is out. Whoever reads next: read the twelve first.*
