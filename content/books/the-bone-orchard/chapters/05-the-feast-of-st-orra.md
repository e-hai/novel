# The Feast of St. Orra

The feast was the last Sunday in November, and by then Cade had been in Orra long enough to know that a feast in Orra was not a celebration.

It began at dusk. The village walked up the lane with lanterns, in silence, in the order they always walked: the oldest first, then the families, then the children, then the priest.

At the gate, Ash gave him the book.

“You bless the harvest,” he said.

“What harvest?”

“The orchard’s.”

The rows were lit with lamps on stakes. The trees were heavy and white, and the bones on the branches caught the light the way frost catches it, and the whole slope looked, for a moment, like a table set for a great number of people.

Cade opened the ledger to the page for the year and read the names, because that was the blessing in Orra: you said the names and you said the state of the tree and you did not say anything else.

*Tull, Martha. 7. Holding.*
*Ash, Peter. 12. Held.*
*Renn, Alice. 3. Green.*

Then he reached the back row and stopped.

Twelve blank tags. Twelve trees. Twelve names that had never been written, and under them a fever that had been taken instead.

The village waited. The lanterns burned. Somebody in the front row began to cry, quietly, in the way people cry when they have been waiting a long time to do it.

Cade put his thumb on the blank page and said the only thing he could say that was true.

“These twelve are not unnamed,” he said. “They are named in the book of the fever, and the fever is in the trees, and I will not read a name that a mother would not sign.”

He closed the ledger.

The silence changed. It was not anger. It was worse: it was relief, the relief of a village that had wanted someone to say it for nineteen years and had never found anyone who would.

Ash took the book out of his hands, gently, the way you take something from a man who has just done a dangerous thing.

“They’ll want you gone by morning,” he said.

“I know.”

“Do you want to go?”

Cade looked at the twelve trees, and at the one he had tagged himself.

“No,” he said.
