# Six Sundays, One Door

They worked it like a job, because Dell had never in his life solved anything with courage and Aubrey had never in his life solved anything without a sheet of paper.

Six Sundays. A plan for each.

The first Sunday they tried the front door at 11:41 and found it locked, and Dell had to run to make the lift. The second Sunday they brought a key from the building’s old office on Calder Street, and the key opened the door, and behind it there was a wall.

The third Sunday they tried the window on the second floor, and the window was painted shut, and Aubrey cut his hand on the frame.

The fourth Sunday Dell delivered the parcel to the green door, and stood there, and said, through the wood: “Marco. If you’re in there, push.”

Nothing pushed.

The fifth Sunday Aubrey sat on the stairs and cried for a while, and then wiped his face and said, “It’s not a door. I’ve been saying it wrong. It’s a signature.”

“What?”

“The building is a signature. It opens for whoever signed. I signed for two and one came out, so it opens for the gap between them. You can’t walk through a gap. You have to fill it.”

Dell thought about that for a long time, standing in a corridor that was beginning to go grey at the edges.

Then he took out the scanner, and put it against the green door, and scanned the door itself instead of the parcel.

The scanner buzzed. The door opened.

Inside was a room with a bed and a chair and a window and a man sitting on the bed with his hands in his lap, and the man was Marco, and Marco was thirty-one and thin and had the same bad haircut he had had at nineteen.

“You took six weeks,” Marco said.

“Six Sundays.”

“Same thing, in here.” Marco stood up. “We’ve got nineteen minutes. What’s the condition?”

“The receipt,” Dell said. “Somebody has to be present to sign it.”
