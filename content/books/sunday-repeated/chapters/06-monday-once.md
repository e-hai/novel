# Monday, Once

The receipt was a piece of paper the size of a bus ticket, and it had three lines on it: ADDRESS, RECIPIENT, PRESENT.

Marco signed it. Then Aubrey signed it, in the box marked *doorkeeper*, which was not a box any scanner had ever printed before, and the paper accepted it without complaint.

They came down the stairs at 11:56 with four minutes left.

The building was already going grey. The lift would not come. They took the stairs, Marco first, Aubrey second, Dell last, and on the second floor landing the wall was thin enough that Dell could see the skip through it.

At 11:58 they came out into the gap between 12 and 16, and the building closed behind them like a book, and the four of them stood on the quay in the ordinary Sunday light with the ordinary Sunday noise, and Aubrey sat down on the kerb and put his head in his hands.

At 23:59 that night, Dell was awake.

The city did not click.

He lay there and waited for it, and it did not come, and at 00:01 he got up and went into the kitchen and made tea, and the tea was the same tea, and the milk was fresh, and on the table there was a parcel with a label on it, and the label said MONDAY.

He delivered sixteen parcels that Monday. Number 14 was not on the list.

Marco was on the list, at an address on Calder Street, and when Dell rang the bell his brother opened the door, thirty-one and thin and alive and holding a phone from 2014 with a cracked corner.

“You look terrible,” Marco said.

“I’ve had a long week.”

They stood there in the doorway for a while. Then Marco stepped back and said, “Come in. I’ll make tea. I’ve got six years of Sundays to tell you about, and I don’t remember any of them.”

Dell went in. Behind him, on the quay, a doorkeeper was walking to the café to order one coffee, and the city was already Tuesday.
