# Attendance Grant

The county paid a penny a day for every child present, and a halfpenny for every child on the roll whether present or not, and the school's whole income came out of those two sums. Nell knew this because the first thing she had been asked to sign was the quarterly return.

The return was a single page. Number on roll. Average attendance. Sum claimed. She filled in twenty-three, twenty-four, and a figure, and the figure was right, and she signed it, and it bothered her for a week.

In the cupboard under the register there was a bundle of letters tied with string, and in the bundle was a letter from the county auditor dated October 1911.

*The return states twenty-four children present. The roll reproduced in the same return contains twenty-three names. The auditor will be obliged to disallow the difference unless a corrected return is received within fourteen days.*

The reply was in a different hand, smaller, and it said: *The roll is correct. No correction is required.* In the margin, in pencil, in the same small hand: *The county is paid for the register, not the room.*

The head then had been Mr. Aldous Praine, who had kept the school for thirty-one years and whose portrait hung behind the door where nobody could see it. Nell found his logbook and read the autumn of 1911. The school had closed for four weeks. Two children had gone to relatives in the north. Then, in November, in his ordinary hand, a line she read three times: *Reopened. Twenty-four present.*

She did the arithmetic that evening at the kitchen table, because doing arithmetic is a way of not thinking. A penny a day, a hundred and eleven years, one child.

It came to something over four hundred pounds, which is not a large sum, which was the point. Nobody audits a number that small. Nobody has to. A number that small can stay in a column for a century without anyone being dishonest about it.

She went to see Mrs. Loam, who was ninety-one and had been a child at the school in 1911, and asked whether she remembered a boy who had come to the door and not been let in.

Mrs. Loam thought about it for long enough that Nell thought she had fallen asleep.

"There was a boy," she said. "On the day we reopened. He stood in the porch with the other two, and Mr. Praine told him he was on the wrong list, and he went away." She looked at the fire. "We were told not to talk about it. So we didn't. And then we were told nothing at all, and we stopped."
