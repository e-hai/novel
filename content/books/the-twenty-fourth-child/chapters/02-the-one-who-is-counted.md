# The One Who Is Counted

He sat at the back, by the window that never opened, and he was only there during roll call.

Nell noticed it on the second morning. When she said the last name on the list the room answered, and then, half a second later, a small voice said, "Here," from the back, in the tone children use when they have already been asked twice.

She looked up. There was a boy in the back row wearing a coat that was too big for him, grey, with the collar turned up. He was not doing anything. He had his hands on the desk.

"That's the one who's counted," said Ivy Mace, who was eleven and had been left in charge of the stove.

"What's his name?"

The children looked at each other in the way children do when an adult has asked something that is not a joke but is also not quite a question. Ivy said, "He doesn't have one."

"Everyone has one."

"He's just the one who's counted," Ivy said, and went back to her sum.

Nell taught the morning. When she looked up again the back row held three children and the chair by the window was empty, the way it had been empty at eight o'clock and would be empty at four. She went and stood by it. The wood was warm.

She tried the roll backwards the next day, from the last name to the first. Twenty-three answered in order, and after the first name, which was now last, the small voice said, "Here," again.

She tried it without the list, calling the children she knew by sight. Twenty-two answered; she missed one who was in the cloakroom. And still, at the end, "Here."

Miss Petty, who came on Thursdays to teach sewing and had been at the school since before the war, said there was nothing wrong with the register. "It has always said twenty-four," she said. "The county has always paid for twenty-four."

"Then one of them has never been on the roll."

"That," said Miss Petty, threading a needle, "would be a matter for the county."
