# Sign-Off

They cut the bulkhead on a Tuesday, with Deol on the torch and Mira holding the extinguisher and Ivo holding a recorder, because whatever was behind it had waited nineteen years and deserved a witness.

M-3 was a corridor and a room and a chair. The room had a rack of dead servers and a desk with a mug on it. The chair had a man in it, in a suit, with a helmet on and the visor down.

He had been dead a long time. The suit had kept him, which is what suits are for, and the station had kept the room cold, which is what stations do when nobody updates a plan.

Ivo did not open the visor. He recorded the room, and the chair, and the desk, and said the date and the time and his own name, which is what a comms officer does when there is nothing left to transmit.

Then he read the log entry aloud, the one that said *one crew recovered*, and added, out loud, the sentence that had never been written:

“Two crew inside. One recovered. One remained. The remaining crew is recovered today, 25 November, by Sarn, Deol, and Mira, of Kestrel.”

They sealed M-3 again that evening, properly this time, with a plate and a name and a date, and Deol ran a cable from the plate to the array so that the station could hear the room if it ever wanted to.

The next morning the burst came at 03:12 and lasted nine minutes exactly, and there was no counting in it, only hiss, then weather, then cargo chatter, then nothing.

Ivo logged it in the ordinary column, and for the first time in four years the *unexplained* box stayed empty.

Then he wrote the last line of the shift, the one every comms officer writes and nobody reads:

*Kestrel, sign-off. All traffic answered. Nothing outstanding.*

Under it he added, because he had learned something on the dark deck:

*A message nobody answers is not a message. It is a noise. Answer the noise.*
