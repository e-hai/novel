# What the Array Heard

Deol took the array apart on the fourteenth day, which is not a thing an engineer does lightly: it costs a shift, and it leaves the station deaf for six hours.

He found the fault in the second feed.

The array was not receiving a signal from outside. It was hearing the station’s own transmissions, delayed and folded, the way a room hears its own echo when the walls are wrong. Nineteen years of them. Every message Kestrel had ever sent, stacked in the cable housing like letters in a drawer.

“It’s a memory,” Deol said. “A bad one.”

“It’s a tape loop.”

“It’s not a loop. It’s the actual traffic. It re-plays, and when it re-plays it wears down, and the wear makes the hiss, and the hiss is nine minutes long because that is how long the emergency broadcast was.”

Ivo looked at the spool in his hand.

“The emergency broadcast from M-3.”

“From M-3.”

They played it at quarter speed. Under the counting, under the hiss, under nineteen years of weather packets and cargo chatter, there was a man’s voice saying the same sentence in a loop, so worn that only the shape of it was left.

*This is Ruk. M-3. Two of us. Door is —*

The rest had been eaten by the loop.

Ivo sat with the tape for a long while.

Then he did the thing that comms officers are trained not to do, which is to transmit on a band that is not a band, to a source that is not a source, in answer to a message that was sent nineteen years ago.

He keyed the mic and said, slowly, with the station clock at his back:

“This is Kestrel. This is Sarn. Anselm Ruk, I have your traffic. I have all of it. The door is in the deck plan. I’m coming down.”

The array hissed. The clock ticked over. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, the pulse stopped.
