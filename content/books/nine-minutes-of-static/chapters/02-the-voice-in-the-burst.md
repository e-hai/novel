# The Voice in the Burst

By the third day the voice had learned names.

It came in the same burst, at the same minute past the hour, nine minutes of hiss with a human shape in the middle of it. It said *Sarn*. It said *Deol*. It said *Mira*, who ran the galley and had never once spoken on the open band. It said *Kestrel*, which was not a name you said on the radio, because the radio was how the station talked to everything that was not the station.

Ivo recorded every burst on three separate tapes and locked them in the drawer with the medical kit, which was the only drawer on Kestrel with a lock.

“It knows the roster,” Deol said.

“Anyone can know the roster.”

“It knows Mira.”

“Mira is on the duty sheet.”

“Mira is on the duty sheet as G-4.” Deol tapped the tape. “It said her name.”

On the fifth day, the voice added a name none of them recognized: *Anselm*.

Ivo checked the manifest twice. Fourteen crew. No Anselm. He checked the old manifests, the ones in the paper binder from before the station kept records digitally, and found an Anselm Ruk, systems technician, assigned to Kestrel for a six-month rotation nineteen years earlier.

The rotation had ended. The binder said so, in a column headed *disembarked*.

“People leave,” Deol said.

“Not always on the shuttle,” Ivo said.

They did not say the rest of it. On Kestrel, a station at the edge of the lanes, you did not need to say the rest of it: modules were sealed when they failed, and crews were small, and paperwork had a way of being completed for you.

That night the burst came three minutes early, and the voice did not count.

It said: *Anselm is still aboard.*
