# A Town Without a Postcode

The mapping office was two floors down and staffed by a man called Rennick who had the manner of someone who had been asked stupid questions for thirty years and had stopped enjoying it.

"Town," he said. "Village. Hamlet. There's a difference and it matters."

"Eleven hundred and forty people."

"Then it's a town, and it would be on the sheet." He typed with two fingers. "Halloway. H-A-L-L-O-W-A-Y. Nothing. Nothing. Nothing." He sat back. "There's a Hallow Farm, forty kilometres east, four residents, sheep. There's Halloway Bank, which is a sandbar. Nothing with a population in four figures."

"Could it have been renamed?"

"Everything gets renamed, eventually. That's the point of records." He looked at her properly then. "Why?"

Ada told him about the run. He listened without interrupting, which she had not expected, and when she finished he took a key from a drawer and said, "Come on."

The archive was in the basement, in a room that had once been a strongroom and still had the door. Rennick pulled a shelf of ledgers: not digital, not scanned, not even indexed, just ninety years of observers' sheets bound in board.

"Your lot kept paper until 1979," he said. "Rainfall, pressure, remarks. The remarks are the interesting bit. Observers wrote remarks when they were bored."

He found 1973 by the third shelf. The sheets were month by month, a page per week, and the handwriting changed in June from a sloped, careful hand to something faster, as if the observer had learned the job and stopped being afraid of it.

HALLOWAY. ELEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY. That was the heading, printed, on every page from January to September.

In September the remarks column said: *Scheme approved. Reservoir to fill October. Station to close end of month. Records to be sent to District.*

"Octob—" Ada stopped. "They drowned it."

"They flooded a valley. There's a difference and it matters," Rennick said, without humour. "The reservoir's been there since seventy-four. I swim in it." He turned the last page of September. There was nothing after it. "Your model's reading the old sheets. Somebody digitised these in the eighties and forgot to tell the model the town stopped existing."

"That's not possible. The model doesn't read archives."

"Then somebody's typing it in every night," Rennick said, and put the ledger back on the shelf with both hands, the way you put a thing back that has already been used once.
