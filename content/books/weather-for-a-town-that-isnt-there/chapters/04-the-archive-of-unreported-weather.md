# The Archive of Unreported Weather

The remark books were the ones nobody had asked for. Rennick had left her the key and a note that said *the ones at the end are loose, don't sneeze*, and she had gone down at eight in the evening with a lamp and a flask and stayed until the building's heating went off.

Halloway's observer had been a woman called H. Vane, and she had written remarks for eleven years. Most of them were weather. *Frost on the flagstones. Wind shifted at four. Two crows on the wall all morning.*

Some of them were not.

*14 March 1968. Mr. Astell's roof. Told him. He says the slates came off before the wind, which is not possible, and I have written what he says.*

*2 June 1969. The school went to the coast. Twenty-nine children and one teacher. I gave them the forecast. They came back dry. I was wrong and they were lucky and I do not like it.*

*9 November 1971. If the Board will not pay for a second gauge I will put one in myself. Mr. Astell says I am not to. Mr Astell does not read this book.*

Ada found the second gauge in the December pages, in remarks two lines long, unbothered: *Gauge 2, orchard end, 0.6 above gauge 1. As usual.*

She turned the pages until September 1973 and read the last three weeks, and then she read them again, and then she went upstairs and stood in the corridor because the corridor had a window and the room did not.

The last remark, dated the twenty-eighth, in the same fast hand:

*They are to open the sluices Monday. I have asked for the sheets to go to District and I have been told they will. I am setting down the total for the year in this book because the other book is to be sent away and I do not trust the post.*

*Twelve hundred and six millimetres. Eleven hundred and forty people. It rained on all of them at one time or another.*

*If anyone reads this later: the valley will be under water by Michaelmas and the water will be called a reservoir and there will be a car park and a tea room. The town was here first. The rain was here first. Neither of those is a remark, but you have kept the book, so write them down.*

The total for the year was what the model had been giving her, hour by hour, without ever being told her name: twelve hundred and six millimetres on a town that had been under fifteen metres of water since before she was born.

And in a box at the bottom of the shelf, still tied with string, were the loose sheets Rennick had warned her about: carbon copies, one per week, transcribed by hand and addressed to District, in a hand Ada had seen every day of her childhood on the backs of envelopes, on shopping lists, on the note her father left when he went out to the river and did not come back.

The copies stopped in September 1973. Her father had been nineteen then, and had not yet met her mother, and had not yet been anything at all.
