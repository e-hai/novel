# Clearing

It took eleven weeks to make Halloway legal. It took one paragraph to make it permanent.

The paragraph went into the station register, which is a document nobody reads until something goes wrong, and read: *Halloway (discontinued 1973; reinstated 2026 as a climatological station reporting from digitised archive and current gauge). Population 1140 at discontinuation. Site now submerged; observations taken at surface of reservoir.*

Rennick objected to the word submerged. Ada kept it.

The gauge went in in April, on a buoy, in the middle of the water, at the point where the map said the school had been. It cost four thousand pounds and reported every hour, and the first thing it reported was nine millimetres, which was two more than the three nearest stations, which was exactly the correction H. Vane had been writing down since 1968.

Paul put the buoy's first month on the wall of the forecast room, next to the district league table for rainfall, where it came third and was mocked for it.

The transcribing stopped in May. Nobody ever found out who had been doing it — the box was empty, the sheets were in the system, complete, all eleven years, and the person who had spent seven months feeding a dead parish into a live model one night at a time simply did not come in again.

Ada thought about that more than she thought about the assessor, or the claim, or the four thousand pounds.

She thought: whoever it was had done the work in the order the model needed it, and had done it without telling anyone, and had stopped the night the station was official. Which is not the behaviour of a vandal. It is the behaviour of an observer who has been relieved.

The last line of H. Vane's last book was a total, and totals are what a station is for. Twelve hundred and six millimetres. Eleven hundred and forty people. It rained on all of them at one time or another.

On the evening the register changed, Ada went up to the reservoir and stood on the dam where the car park is, and looked at the water doing what water does in a valley, which is to lie flat and take the shape of whatever used to be underneath it.

The forecast for the following day was nine millimetres, clearing by afternoon. It was right. It had been right every night for seven months, in a place with no postcode, and nobody had been watching.
