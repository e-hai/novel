# What the Barometer Remembers

The assessor came on a Tuesday with a folder and a request that had been through three committees.

"We have a claim," she said. "Storm, February. A property at Halloway. Insured value, listed buildings, the whole thing. The claimant has supplied a weather report. Our reinsurer will not accept it because the location does not resolve."

"You want a certified record for a place that's under a reservoir."

"I want a certified record for the weather that damaged the property," the assessor said. "The property is at Halloway. The property is also, apparently, at the bottom of a reservoir. Both of those can be true if the policy was written in 1972."

Paul had been in the room for four minutes and had said nothing. Now he said, "If we certify it, we certify that we forecast for a town that isn't on any map."

"If you don't certify it," the assessor said, "the reinsurer pays nothing, the claimant loses a house he can't see, and it goes to an ombudsman who will ask why a public body's model has been issuing nightly forecasts for a drowned parish since March."

Nobody said anything for a while. The heating clicked.

"It's been doing it since March," Paul said, eventually, and Ada understood that he had been watching it too, and had been watching it longer than she had, and had been not filing it for the same reason she had been writing it on the backs of sheets.

"Then somebody's been feeding it," the assessor said. "Who?"

Ada thought about the shelf in the basement, and the string-tied box, and a nineteenyear-old with a good hand who had copied rainfall onto carbons for a District that had already decided to drown the valley.

"There's a set of sheets," she said. "Nineteen seventy-three. They were transcribed by hand. Somebody has been re-entering them, a few at a time, for months, in the right order, at the right speed — one night's worth per night. As if the observer were still on duty."

"That's a person," the assessor said.

"That's a person," Ada agreed.

Paul took his coat off the back of the chair and put it on. "Right," he said. "Then we certify it. And then we find out who's been keeping the station open, and we give them a proper logbook and a proper number, because I am not having a dead parish out-observed by a temp on a night shift."

The assessor wrote something down. At the door she turned. "Miss Kell. The claimant's name is Vane. Does that mean anything to you?"

"It's an old local name," Ada said, and did not say the rest, which was that it was also the name at the bottom of every carbon copy in the box, and that whoever was still transcribing those sheets at three in the morning had learned the handwriting from the same person she had.
