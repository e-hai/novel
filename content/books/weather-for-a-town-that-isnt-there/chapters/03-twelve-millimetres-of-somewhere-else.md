# Twelve Millimetres of Somewhere Else

For six nights the station kept arriving at three o'clock, and for six nights Ada wrote it down.

She compared it to the three gauges nearest the old valley: Carrow Bridge, Fenn's Weir, Kettle Row. Halloway never matched any of them exactly. It never missed by more than a fifth of a millimetre either.

"Look at this," she said, on the seventh night, laying the sheets on Paul's desk. "It's not copying a station. It's interpolating. It's sitting in the middle of the three and giving us the average, and the average is wrong by four-tenths, consistently, in the same direction, which means it's not an average, it's a measurement."

Paul read the sheets with the flat patience of a man who had once been interested in weather.

"Ada. You've been here two years. You know what a ghost station is."

"A station in the list with no observer."

"This is a ghost station in the list with no observer, no location, no postcode, and no paperwork, and you want me to open a file." He tapped the column of figures. "Twelve millimetres. Last hour. Where did it rain twelve millimetres last hour? Nowhere. It rained nine, eleven, and fifteen. Twelve is the middle. Twelve is what you get when a machine averages three numbers."

"Then why is it always low by four-tenths?"

"Because gauges read high in wind," Paul said, and looked pleased with himself, and then looked at the sheet again for a while.

On the eighth night the model put Halloway at 0.0 millimetres, and the three gauges read 0.0 as well, and the district had the driest hour of the month.

On the ninth night it rained over the reservoir and the model gave Halloway twelve, and the three gauges gave nine, eleven, and fifteen, and Ada sat in the dark room with her coat still on and understood that a number could be a location. Something was raining somewhere between Kettle Row and Fenn's Weir, at a place with a name in a ledger, and the only instrument that could see it was a forecasting model that had been told, by somebody, that the place was still there.

She rang Paul at ten past three.

"Do not open a file," he said, before she had spoken. "I'm asking you. Leave it. It's four-tenths of a millimetre on a night shift. It's not a finding, it's a rounding error with a postcode."

"It hasn't got a postcode. That's the whole—"

"I know," Paul said. "That's why I'm asking."
