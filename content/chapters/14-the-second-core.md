# The Second Core

They opened it on a Thursday, at the hour when the lane is emptiest and the shop smells most of its own dust.

Mara set the lamp at the end of the bench instead of beside it, so the light came in from the side and did not flatten the core. Elias had not thought of doing that in eleven years. He watched her do it and felt the small, specific shame of being taught something by a person who had learned it from him.

"Ready," she said.

He seated the core.

The first thing was the cold. Not the cold of a room but the cold of a drawer — a narrow, man-made cold. Then the scene resolved, and it was not the kitchen.

It was an archive.

A long corridor with a colourless floor, and along both walls the numbered panels the Ward used instead of shelves. Each panel had a small inset light, and each light was the same dull amber, which meant each record was sealed and each seal was holding. At the far end, a door stood open. Blue. Not the blue of the door in the stranger's damaged engram, not the blue the loom had learned to offer. This blue was older, cheaper, the blue of a municipal door that had been repainted over a blue that was almost the same.

"That's the ninth floor," Mara said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I've stood in it." She did not move. "That's the intake corridor. Panels on the left are depositions. Panels on the right are reviews. The open door at the end is where they take you when the record is contested."

Elias ran the engram forward. The corridor advanced smoothly, the way an official memory advances — no drift, no breath, nothing that had been lived. A figure came out of the door at the end and walked toward them, and the figure was a woman in a grey coat with a lanyard, and the lanyard had a number on it that Elias could not read.

She stopped in front of them. She looked directly at the viewer, the way people do in intake rooms, where the camera is a formality and everyone knows where it is.

"Depositor present," she said. "Record opened. Second signature required."

Then she held out a stylus, and the view turned, and Elias saw a hand take the stylus, and the hand was his.

He felt Mara go still beside him. He felt his own hand, on the bench, make the shape of holding a stylus with nothing in it.

The signature appeared in the air in front of the panel, written in a hand that leaned the way his leaned, that pressed hard at the start of the E and let go at the end of the n. Under it, a date. Not 24 November. The tenth.

"That's not—" he said, and stopped, because *that's not my signature* was a sentence that could be checked, and he did not want it checked.

"It's the tenth," Mara said. "You were here on the tenth. You had the letter on the—"

"The letter came on Tuesday."

"Then you were here before it came."

He ran it back. The corridor reversed, the woman walked backwards into the blue door, the door closed, the panels dimmed, the cold narrowed down to the first frame. He ran it forward again, slowly, looking for the join. There was no join. An engram that had been edited shows its edits the way a wall shows where it has been patched, and this one was smooth from the first frame to the last.

"Somebody built this," he said. "From the log. From the loom's record of the repair."

"Then they built it before they wrote to you."

She was right, and it was the kind of right that makes a room smaller. He sat back. The lamp at the end of the bench threw his shadow across the counter, and the shadow had its own edge, and he looked at it instead of at her.

"Sealed cores are sealed for a reason," Mara said. "Not to keep the client's memory in. To keep the archive's memory out."

He turned the core over in his hand. It was warm now, the way cores get warm. On the sleeve, in his own small label hand, was the number of the repair and the date of the original intake, and nothing else.

"There's a second signature in there," he said. "In my hand."

"Yes."

"And a room I don't remember being in."

"Yes."

"Then either I went, or someone wants the record to say I went."

Mara took the core from him and set it back in its sleeve. She did it carefully, folding the paper at the corners the way the old suppliers folded it, and she did not look at him while she did it.

"Both can be true," she said. "That's the trouble with records. They don't have to choose."
