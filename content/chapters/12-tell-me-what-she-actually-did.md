# Tell Me What She Actually Did

Rain had been falling since before the shutters went up. It came in flat along Cinder Lane and struck the awning with the sound of grit thrown against sheet iron. The smell followed it under the door — hot metal, rising from the corrugated roofs as the water hit them. Elias had stopped hearing the sound. He had never stopped smelling it.

The old man came in just after eleven.

He put the envelope on the counter, then the square of cloth, then the coins in a row. Three movements in the old order. His coat still had three different buttons; the top one was new, plain and brighter than the rest, sewn on in a hurry by someone who had not had time to match it.

"It isn't right," he said.

Elias looked at the envelope. "The repair?"

"The repair's all right. It's her."

He had expected the visit for two days, ever since Mara's folder went out of the shop and left a fourth line on the rule sheet. Expecting it and preparing for it were not the same. He opened the drawer for an intake slip. It caught, as it had since he put the second core in it, and he lifted the front edge to clear it. The sealed core lay in its paper sleeve. Beside it, the mother's core, face up. On top, the rule sheet: three lines in his hand and a smaller slanting line in hers — *Mara, witness, named and present. Old licence 4-119.* The blue-edged sleeve that had held the sealed core was not in the drawer. It sat on the shelf behind the loom, where he had left it the day before. He took one slip and pushed the drawer shut.

"Sit," he said.

The old man took the stool without being asked, the way he had the first time.

Elias seated the engram. The kitchen came up in thin morning light: the patched lower pane, the grey cardigan, the white cup with the dark line at the handle. He ran it to the last intact frame and held it. The woman's left hand lifted. A breath entered the track and the recording went pale.

The loom offered its bridge. It had learned to place a blue door at the end of a damaged corridor, a door into a white room with a numbered panel — an ordinary room that thousands of people had walked through. He left the door shut. There was nothing in it for this client.

"Again," the old man said. "Just once more. I'll pay."

Elias kept his hand off the controls. "Tell me what she actually did."

"She laughed."

"Tell me what she did. Not what came out of it."

The old man's thumb found the cloth. "She burnt her sleeve. I've told you. Twice."

"You told me what happened." Elias turned the old intake slip so he could read it. *Recollection given now, not a recovered recording.* Under that line were the stove, the green stitches, the man's own account of being frightened. "The machine can only use what's in the core. Everything else has to come from you. It won't come out clean."

"You people always say that," the old man said. "You can keep what happened. You can't keep what it was like."

Elias wrote the sentence at the top of the fresh slip, then underlined it. *You can save the facts, you can't save the feeling.* He had put a smaller version of it on receipts for years without once writing it as an instruction.

"I'm not going to fill the gap," he said. "I'll take out the clean sleeve I put in the first time, and I'll leave the missing sound marked. What you tell me goes in the margin, with your name and today's date. Beside the record, not inside it. Nobody will be able to mistake it for something the machine made."

"Is that a repair?"

"It's a record of a repair that didn't work." He capped the pen. "Tell me and I'll write. If you'd rather I did it the old way, say so now."

The old man looked at the suspended woman, then at the cloth on the counter. "She was watching the pan," he said, and stopped. "No. She was watching me. I don't know which."

"Which hand held the cup?"

"Left." He frowned. "No — if the burn was on her left sleeve, she'd have turned with her left. So the cup was in her right." He looked at Elias to see whether he had got it right. "I can't tell you. I'm making it up as I go."

"That's the part I can't make for you," Elias said.

He wrote it down: *Cup — hand uncertain. Turn — toward the man or the bread cart. Client cannot fix which.* The man corrected him on the last words. Not gone: heard, unidentified. Elias crossed out *gone* and wrote *words not recoverable; sound of them remembered*. The ink sat wet where his hand had rested on the paper.

"Did she say anything before the break?"

"She said my name. I think." His thumb pressed the scorched corner of the cloth. "She used to say it when I did something stupid. Not angry. Like the time she—" He broke off. "I don't know what she said."

Elias wrote that too. He did not tidy the order of it, or move the uncertain parts after the certain ones, or remove the client's correction of his own first answer. On the loom the kitchen waited at the lifted hand.

He took out the clean sleeve first. The cuff came back scorched and irregular, the damage flag showing where he had once suppressed it. Then he opened the pale interval and set a narrow note along its edge: the man's name, the date, the words *given now, not recovered*. The seam stayed visible. It ran a little unevenly across the sound track, a ridge where one account met another, the way the green stitches had crossed the grey ones on the cloth.

He did not score it. He no longer kept the number on a receipt.

The woman turned. Her head went a fraction too far and came back. The cuff showed its dark patch. No sound came out of the engram. The old man watched it once, then a second time, and on the third he did not ask Elias to stop.

"She's not careful," he said. "That's her."

Elias put the coins back in their row. "I can't take these for that."

"I don't care where it went," the old man said. "In the machine or beside it. She turned and she said—"

Elias did not finish it for him. He pushed the drawer shut with his hip and left it shut. Out front there was still no sign above the shutter, only the two bare brackets where one had been. The rain thinned to a vapour that rose off the awning and smelled of hot metal.

The old man started again from the beginning, the way people do when they have found the place where the telling comes alive. "She turned, and she said—"
