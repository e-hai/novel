# The Rule, Written Down

Rain started before the shutter was fully up. It came flat along Cinder Lane and hit the metal awning with a sound like handfuls of grit. The smell followed through the open door — hot iron, the smell the trade had put into him. Elias lifted the archive packet off the till to reach the drawer.

He had stood it there the night before so that morning would make him move it. It worked. The packet had his name, the shop address, and a request to reopen the classification of patient reports. A clerk would have to answer a named claimant. An unanswered claim might bring an inspector to an unlicensed shop. He had written those words and then left the packet where it stood. Still standing.

He opened the drawer. The first core lay in its paper sleeve, sealed, with the date on it. Beside it, the second core, labelled for the mother, face up. Then the two folded sheets.

He unfolded them on the counter. **Do not repair your own engrams.** Under it, in the same hand but a later day: **Do not make a whole where two people were.** The paper had been folded so long that the creases had gone pale and soft. His own handwriting, and he could read it as easily as a stranger could. That was the trouble. There was nothing on the sheet that said what it was for, or when, or who had agreed to it. A rule written by one person, kept in a drawer, witnessed by nobody. He had been the only reader and the only judge of it for years.

He thought about the packet. A claimant had to be named. A clerk had to answer. Nothing in the archive could be settled by one person alone in a room; it moved from a desk to a desk, and each desk wanted a name. He had understood that when he wrote the packet. He had understood it again last night, when Mara described the witness protocol he had designed — a second person beside the loom so the fill could not take over. He had built the safeguard for other people and kept none for himself.

A client came in at nine, a man with a damp coat and an engram of his father's last winter. Nothing difficult. A clean-up of a recording that had gone soft at the edges. Elias set it on the loom and worked. The man watched his hands.

"My sister says you can't save the feeling," the man said. "The clinic says they give you back what happened. I keep the facts. So which is it?"

Elias did not look up. "Both are true. The slogan leaves out the part your sister noticed."

He closed the fill. "The feeling isn't extra. It's what tells you which facts mattered. Your father's winter is complete. It won't tell you what it was like to be him."

The man took that as a price and paid it. He left the coins in a row on the counter, the way people left things they did not want to pick up again.

When the shop was empty, Elias put his own core on the reader.

He did not mean to open it. He meant only to look at the label. The loom recognised the signature in the header — his — and offered, as it always did, the bridge: a plausible interval where the fracture was. He could see it waiting. A room, a folder, a younger man who kept his nerve. The life in which he had refused the pen and been right all along. Eighteen seconds of it, once. He knew the shape of it without running it.

He took the core off the reader.

The rule had kept him from this. It had also kept him company. He had told himself it was protection; what it had actually done was give him somewhere to be alone with a record nobody else had to see. He had preserved the fracture perfectly. He had never once lived with it in front of another person. Preserving a thing and living with it were not the same.

He picked up the pen and unfolded the sheet again. He did not cross out the first two lines. He wrote beneath them, in the same hand, slowly:

**Do not work on your own memory alone. A second person must be present and named on the sheet.**

He read all three lines. The first two were still true. He had no intention of repairing the sealed core. The second line still held. The third line did not cancel them; it added the one thing they lacked — someone who could read the sheet besides himself.

He put the two cups on the counter. On the message pad he wrote Mara's name and the hour, so the sequence would be right. Then he telephoned the number she had left with the shop years ago. She answered on the second ring.

"The loom is free," he said. "If you want to sit with me, this afternoon."

He did not explain the rest. There was a short silence, and he heard her set something down — a cup, or perhaps the folder.

He hung up. Outside, the rain thinned to a vapour that rose off the awning and smelled of hot metal. On the counter the rule sheet lay flat between the two cups, three lines in one hand, the folds gone soft. He pictured a folder with a blue edge being laid into a bag across town, and moved the spare cup a little nearer the loom.
