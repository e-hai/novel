# A Blue Door in Someone Else's Memory

The boy was nineteen and had been in an accident that the clinic called minor and his mother called the end of his life. What he had lost was a year, compressed to a smear around its edges. The clinic had repaired it and sent him home and he had come here instead, because the repair did not match the stories his mother told.

"There's a door," he said. "Blue. In all of it. She says there was no door."

Elias ran the engram. A room, a corridor, a stairwell — ordinary, worn, the angles of a cheap building. And in the middle of it, a door the color of a sky just before rain. The boy was right. It appeared in three separate fragments, always the same blue, always the same worn place at the handle where many hands had been.

It was not a memory of a door. It was a signature.

Elias had seen that blue before. He had seen it in his own erased engram, in the moment he had spent the last weeks refusing to re-enter. He had assumed it was a place from the lab. But a place did not follow you into other people's memories.

He worked the boy's engram carefully, keying in small fills at the edges, and as he worked he thought about the loom. His was old, unlicensed, built from parts that had been legal in another decade. Inside the casing, beneath the feed assembly, there was a plate he had never cleaned because the manual said it did not need cleaning. He had never read the number on it. He had never had reason to.

When the boy left — repaired, satisfied, his mother waiting outside with a face made of relief — Elias went behind the loom and unscrewed the panel.

There was a serial number. Not a random one. A sequence he recognized with a part of himself that did not need thinking: the early format, the one his team had used before the clinics standardized everything. Stamped into the metal, deep, as if someone had wanted it found.

Under it, smaller: a single word. *Witness.*

He had built this loom himself. He knew he had. And he had never seen that word.

He put the panel back and sat at the counter in the quiet, the rain on the awning, the blue door still somewhere behind his eyes. It occurred to him, slowly and without comfort, that a person could erase a memory of being in a room — but the room did not therefore become empty. Someone else had been there. The door was in the boy's memory too, which meant the boy had been there, or something of the boy had.

Or the door was not in any place at all. It was a thing the looms put there.

He took out the sealed envelope the woman had left, the one he had promised to give to the next client who came asking to forget. He did not open it. He only held it for a moment, feeling its weight, and thought: *the facts survive. The feeling does not.* And underneath, the newer thought, the one he had earned: *and someone decides which facts.*

Outside, the rain kept at the metal. He closed the drawer on the envelope and the rule together, and went back to the loom, and this time he read the serial number twice.
