# Fill Ordered in Bulk

The warehouse was outside the Ward, past the roundabout where the tram lines stop, in a building that had been a refrigerated store and still was, in the way that a building never stops being what it was built for.

Mara had the address from the courier's docket. Elias had the docket because he had kept it, which is a thing people do when they have started suspecting paper.

The man at the gate wore a lanyard with no name on it. "Trade?"

"Repairman. Licence 4-119."

"Fill?"

"Fill."

He let them in. The cold came out of the loading bay in a single breath, and inside it was the smell that fill has, which is the smell of clean linen that has been ironed too hot.

Bricks were stacked on pallets, twelve to a layer, eight layers high. Each brick was wrapped in paper, and each paper had a form printed on it in small type, and the form had fields.

Elias took one and read it while Mara talked to the gate man about invoices.

*Material: connective fill, grade C. Emotional inertness: within specification. Source: voluntary donation, compensated. Donor reference: 4471-B. Hours contributed: 412. Certification: single signature.*

"Read that," he said, and gave the paper to Mara.

She read it twice. "Four hundred and twelve hours."

"Of what?"

"Doesn't say." She turned it over. "It says *childhood*, in the small print. Look. *Donor contributed 412 hours of childhood recollection, ages six to eleven, compensated at the standard rate.*"

Elias took the paper back. The standard rate was printed at the bottom, in the same small type, and it was not a large number, and it was not a small number either. It was the kind of number that a person might agree to in a room with a pen in their hand.

"Who certifies this?"

"The warehouse. One signature." Mara pointed. "There's a box for a second. It's empty."

"Empty's fine. Empty means it never got opened."

They walked along the pallets. Every brick had a form. Every form had a donor reference, and hours, and a single signature, and an empty box. Elias counted the references as he went, not because counting helped, but because the alternative was standing still.

At the end of the aisle, a woman in an apron was sealing a pallet with a hand press. She looked up.

"You're the shop," she said. "The one the letter went to."

"News travels."

"News travels at the rate of the invoice." She pressed the seal and held it. "You want to buy a brick or you want to look at the forms?"

"Both."

"Then buy one and look at the rest. The looking's free. The brick's not."

He bought a brick. Grade C, standard rate, single signature. She wrote the invoice by hand, and while she wrote it she talked, because people who work in cold rooms talk to keep their hands steady.

"Donor 4471-B came in with a folder," she said. "They all do. They bring photographs and they want to be sure the hours are the right ones. She had a photograph of a back garden with a swing in it. She said the swing was the thing. She said she'd sell the swing and keep the rest."

"Did she?"

"No. The form doesn't do single objects. It does hours. You sell by the hour or you don't sell." The woman tore the invoice off the pad. "She cried in the car park afterward. I saw her from the window. Not because of the money. Because she couldn't remember what the swing looked like by the time she got outside."

Elias put the invoice in his coat.

"You going to use that brick?" the woman asked.

"I don't know."

"Then you wasted your money."

"Maybe." He picked the brick up. It was heavier than it looked, and colder, and inside it, evenly distributed, was four hundred and twelve hours of a stranger's back garden.

Mara held the door. Outside, the daylight was thin and the roundabout was full of cars going nowhere in particular.

"You can't refuse a material and still use the machine," she said.

"No."

"So what are you going to do with it?"

He looked at the brick in his arms. On the paper, the empty box for the second signature looked like a window in a wall that had been bricked up from the inside.

"Note where it came from," he said.

They took the tram back into the Ward. The brick sat on his knees the whole way, and by the time they reached Cinder Lane it had warmed to the temperature of his hands, which is the only kind of warmth that fill ever has.
