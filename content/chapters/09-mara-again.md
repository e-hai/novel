# Mara, Again

Mara came just before closing, carrying a folder with a blue edge.

Elias knew her before she spoke. The years had made her smaller in the shoulders. Her hair had gone the color of ash, and her face had the light, practiced calm of a moderate repair. She had kept enough feeling to find the shop. He wondered what she had left in order to manage that.

"You look tired," she said.

"I've been busy."

"With complaints."

"With records."

Mara set the folder on the counter and did not move her hand from it. "Halloway called me."

Elias said nothing.

"He said you were offered a room."

"And you came to tell me to take it."

"I came because you didn't." She looked around the shop: the two cups, the old loom, the drawer that would not close easily since he had put the second core in it. "You always did make a clutter of a principle."

He wanted to ask her why she was there, in the way a man asks a question when he already knows the answer and needs to hear whether the other person will lie. Instead he made tea. He took down two cups. Mara watched him do it without gratitude.

"I need a repair," she said.

"Nobody sends you."

"I'm not here as a friend. I'm here as a client."

He looked at the folder. "What happened?"

"The release day. The soldier. The amended wording. My part in it." Her voice was even. "I want it smoothed. I can't work with the seam anymore."

Elias had imagined this request from her for years, though never in her voice. In his imagination she had arrived younger and angry, demanding that he restore her confidence. The real woman sat quietly with her gloves folded in her lap.

"You know what I filed," he said.

"Yes."

"You know what it says."

"Mostly."

"Then why ask me?"

"Because you're the only repairman I trust who still reads what he signs."

He set the loom running.

Her engram opened on the laboratory corridor. It was the same corridor from his own damaged record, but lit from the opposite end. Mara stood outside the meeting room with the folder pressed against her side. She was younger. Her face was not calm then. It was frightened and severe at once.

The door was blue.

Elias kept his hand over the controls. In his own memory the door had been a place value, a thing the machine used to bridge a gap. Here it was a door someone stood beside, choosing whether to enter.

The scene moved to the table. The director. The soldier's empty release sheet. Mara's younger hand turned a page, stopped, and turned it back.

In the surviving edge of Elias's memory, he had seen that hand finish a question. He had never known what the question was.

Now he did.

Mara had written: *Will the patient be told which responses were generated?*

The director had said, "That is a disclosure question, not a clinical one."

Mara had said, "Then put it in the record as a disclosure question."

In the engram, a clerk took the page from her. The page came back with a narrower margin. The question was still there, but the word *generated* had become *supported*. The word *patient* had become *client*. The sentence now asked whether a client would be informed of supported responses. It was a question that could be answered yes without saying anything at all.

Elias stopped the replay.

"You knew," he said.

"I wrote it."

"You knew what they would do to it."

"I hoped they wouldn't."

"You put my name under it."

Mara looked at the frozen scene. "I put your name where they expected a name. If it had been mine, they would have replaced me by Friday."

The room seemed very small. He could hear water moving in the kettle as it cooled.

"After the meeting," he said, "you came to my room."

"Yes."

"You told me to leave the folder."

"Yes."

"You said you would handle it."

"I said I would make it stop."

"How?"

Mara did not answer at once. On the loom, the younger Mara stood in the corridor beside the blue door, holding the folder with both hands. The older Mara watched her with no expression at all.

"I used the witness protocol," she said. "Do you remember that? It was supposed to let a second person steady a damaged record so the fill wouldn't take over. You designed it. I asked for one session. You were exhausted. You agreed."

Elias felt the edge of the counter under his palm.

"You went into my memory," he said.

"I went into the record. There was a difference then, or I told myself there was."

"What did you take out?"

"The room. My hand on the page. My voice telling you to sign because the alternative was worse." She looked at him now. "I took out enough that you could sleep. That was all I promised myself. I never promised you truth."

He remembered the blank interval. The three plausible continuations the loom had offered him. He had refused them because they were invented. He had not imagined that the absent figure had been standing beside him with a folder.

"Was it protection?" he asked. "Or your career?"

"Yes."

"Mara."

"Yes," she said again, and this time there was something like pain in it. "I told myself you were the better repairman. You were the one who could still be shocked by it. I had a child. I had a mortgage. I had already signed two earlier versions. Do you want the honest order of reasons? I can give it to you, but it won't make the act smaller."

He looked at the blue door in her memory. It opened inward. Beyond it was only the laboratory, a white wall, a numbered panel, a fire exit sign with one letter gone. No hidden room. No second machine. Just a corridor that thousands of people had walked through and one man had been made not to remember.

"You're grey," he said.

"Lightly. I left myself the seam. I thought that would be enough."

"It wasn't."

"No."

He could smooth the incident. He knew how. A few careful fills, a bridge over the witness session, a clean record in which Mara had merely observed and failed to stop what happened. She would leave with a past she could repeat without flinching. He could charge her nothing, or charge her the same as anyone else. It would not change what the fill was.

"Do you want me to take it out?" he asked.

Mara's hands tightened once, then relaxed. "I want you to tell me what it's worth."

"The memory?"

"The truth. Now that you have it. Is it worth losing the only person who can put your case in front of the archive? Is it worth making me remember what I did every time I open a loom? Is it worth a story the clinic will call a personal grievance between two former colleagues?"

Elias did not answer.

That was the cruelty of it. The facts were no longer missing. He had the page, the altered wording, the witness protocol, the blue door, and her confession. None of it carried the dead weight of a decision. That part was still his.

He stopped the loom and took the engram off the reader.

"I won't repair it tonight," he said.

Mara stood. "Then what are you doing?"

"Listening."

She laughed once, softly. "You were always slow."

He put the folder back into her hands. At the door she turned, and for a moment he saw the younger woman from the corridor in the set of her jaw: frightened, severe, certain that one small erasure would be cheaper than the truth.

She did not ask him to forgive her.

"Decide what it is worth," she said. "And then live with the price."
