# The Joint, in Writing

The sign went back up on a Sunday, in weather that had decided to be kind.

Elias did it himself, with the two brackets and a borrowed ladder, and Mara held the ladder because he had asked her to, and because holding a ladder is the kind of thing that gets written into a rule sheet if you do it often enough.

The sign was the same sign. The paint on the lettering had gone a little chalky at the edges, and the awning had a new seam where the canvas had been patched, and he had not repainted either, because a shop that repaints itself every year looks like a shop that is hiding something.

When he came down, the boy in the green coat was standing on the lane with his folder under his arm.

"I went up the stairs," the boy said.

"And?"

"There's a room. It's not a room. It's a landing with a window and a chair." He shifted. "My grandmother's chair. The one she died in."

Elias waited.

"I stood in the doorway for a long time," the boy said. "Then I went in and sat in the chair."

"And?"

"It didn't hurt." He said it as if reporting a fault. "It's just a chair. It's got a dent in the seat where she sat."

"Then it isn't the last thing anymore."

"No." The boy looked at the folder. "I want to keep the receipt, though."

"What receipt?"

"The one you gave me. The slip." He held it up. It had been folded and unfolded enough to go soft at the crease. "It says *ask what is through the door*. I want to keep it."

"Keep it," Elias said. "It's yours. I don't keep receipts anymore."

The boy went off down the lane, and the bread cart came around the corner at the same moment, and for a second the two of them were in the same frame, the boy with his green coat and the cart with its bell, and Elias stood under his own sign and watched them both.

Inside, the bench was clean. The lamp was at the end of it, where Mara had put it in August and where it had stayed. The loom was ticking as it cooled, unevenly, the way it always had.

The rule sheet lay flat on the counter, and it was a new sheet, because the old one had run out of room.

Five lines now. The first four in his hand, the fifth in hers.

*Two accounts, both kept. The core is not the record. The repair is.*
*Joint, not seam. If it is named before the loom, it can be named after.*
*Second licence holder: 6-204. Named before. Present at the loom.*

He read the three lines and then he read the two above them, which were older and worse and still true, and then he turned the sheet over.

On the back, in pencil, was the sentence he had written on the day the letter came: *The repair was attended.* Under it, the crossed-out word. Under that, the number of the review, and the date, and the name of the inspector, because a finding is a thing that happened and should be written where a person can find it.

He put the sheet in the drawer. The drawer did not catch. He had cleared it in September, and the second core had gone back to the archive with Ilse Renn, and the drawer had been empty since, and it still caught sometimes, out of habit.

Mara came in from the back with a cup in each hand. "The boy?"

"He went up the stairs."

"Good." She set the cups down. "There's a woman coming at two. She wants her sister's voice put back."

"Put back or taken out?"

"Put back. Her sister's been dead six years and she's forgotten how she said her name."

Elias took the intake slip and wrote the time. Then he wrote, under it, in the small cautious hand he used for labels: *Two accounts. Joint named before.*

"Named before," Mara said, reading it upside down. "That's the rule now."

"That's the rule now."

Outside, the rain started again, and it came in flat along the lane and struck the awning, and the sound of it was the sound of grit thrown against sheet iron, which was the sound the lane made when it wanted to be remembered.

The sign swung a little on its brackets and settled. Under it, the shop was open.
