# Two at the Loom

Mara arrived at two with the folder under her arm and rain on her coat. She took the stool without being asked and set the folder flat on her knee.

Elias put the second cup where it could be reached but did not pour yet. The rule sheet lay between them on the counter, three lines in his hand, and beneath them a space he had left that morning. *A second person must be present and named on the sheet.* Named. He had written the word and then left the blank.

"Tea," she said. It was not a request or a refusal. It was the shape of the afternoon.

He poured.

Outside, rain came flat along the lane and struck the awning with the sound of grit thrown against sheet iron. The smell came in with it — hot metal, the smell that had followed him through thirty years of other people's recollections. Mara lifted her head. "It still smells that way."

"It's the roof."

"I know what it is."

He set the sealed core on the reader. It had lain in its paper sleeve since the night he took it off the machine. On the back, in his own hand: *Don't open this, Elias.* He had written that, or someone had, and the certainty no longer mattered the way it had.

"The witness protocol," he said. "You asked for one session."

"One session."

"You designed half of it, I designed the other. Between us we took out a room and a hand on a page and a voice telling me to sign." He kept his hand off the controls. "I want them back in the record. Not in me. In the record."

"There was a difference," she said.

"There is."

The loom was idle, its surface dark. The protocol let a second person hold the edges while the first went in, so the fill could not wander into the gaps and invent a life. He had built it for clients. Mara had used it on him. Now they would use it properly, with both names on the sheet.

He opened the folder. The page inside carried the altered wording — the question about which responses were generated narrowed to *supported*, the *patient* become *client*. He had copied it once, years ago, from a record he was not allowed to keep. Seeing it again brought none of the old shock. It brought the smaller thing: the weight of a decision made in a room he could not enter alone.

"Ready," she said.

He seated the core and opened his own record at the fracture. The loom offered the bridge as it always did — eighteen seconds of a plausible room, a younger man who kept his nerve, the life in which he had refused the pen and been right all along. He pushed it aside. The gap stayed open, pale and patient.

"Hold it."

Mara put her hand on the loom's rim. She did not touch the controls. She held the frame still, the way one person holds a ladder for another. In the record the vacancy stopped widening.

Then he ran her engram on the second channel. The corridor came up lit from the opposite end. The blue door. Mara outside the meeting room, younger, frightened and severe at once, the folder pressed to her side. In his own memory the door had been a place value, a thing the machine used to bridge a gap. Here it was a door someone stood beside, choosing whether to enter.

"Say what you remember," he said. "Not what you filed."

"I asked them to put the question in the record as a disclosure question."

"Say the rest."

"I put your name where they expected a name. If it had been mine, they would have replaced me by Friday." Her voice was even. "I told myself you were the better repairman. You were the one who could still be shocked by it."

The two records lay side by side. His: a table, a director, an empty release sheet, a hand finishing a question he had never heard. Hers: the same table from the other end, the page going out and coming back with a narrower margin. Neither was whole. Played alone, either one would be filled with something plausible and clean.

"You signed it," he said.

"I signed two earlier versions first. I signed this one because the alternative was a successor who would not ask the question at all."

"I signed the last one."

"Yes."

The truth was not waiting in the gap. That was what he had expected, and what had kept him from the drawer for twenty years — the belief that somewhere inside the missing interval there was a fact that would make him innocent, or a fact that would make him guilty. There was neither. There was a room, and a decision, and two people who had known what the grey would do to the clients who came after them, and had sold the looms anyway. Both names on the page. Both hands on the folder.

"Fill it," Mara said.

"You asked me to take it out last night."

"Last night I wanted it smooth." She kept her hand on the rim. "This isn't smooth. I can feel you doing it."

He turned to the controls. This was the part of the protocol he had written and never tested: the fill need not come from the machine. A witness who had been in the room could hold the missing interval steady while the repairman keyed in what the second record supplied. Not a generated bridge. A remembered one. It would carry her fear and his exhaustion and the exact click of a pen on paper. A seam made of another person's recollection — imperfect, and feeling.

He keyed it in slowly. The room came back in two halves that did not quite meet. The younger Mara turned from the blue door. The younger Elias set a pen down on the release sheet. Between them the gap closed with a small visible unevenness — not the flat calm of synthetic tissue, but the slight ridge of two accounts laid over one another. The loom scored it. He did not look at the number.

On the wall of the record the blue door opened inward. Beyond it was the laboratory: a white wall, a numbered panel, a fire exit sign with one letter gone. An ordinary room. Thousands of people had walked through it. One man had been made not to remember it, and now remembered it with someone else's help, and the remembering had a seam in it the way a mended coat has a seam.

He took his hand off the controls. Mara took hers off the rim.

"I still can't tell which of us was right," she said.

"Neither of us was."

"No." She let out a breath. "But it holds."

He looked at the rule sheet. Beneath his third line, in the space he had left, there was a fourth line in a hand that was not his — smaller, slanted, written with the pen she carried in the folder. *Mara — witness, named and present. Old licence 4-119.* Under it, the date. She had signed it while he poured the tea. He had not heard the pen move.

Outside, the rain thinned to a vapour that rose off the awning and smelled of hot metal. He put the two cups in the sink and left them there. The core stayed on the reader a moment longer than it needed to; then he returned it to its paper sleeve, and this time he did not put the sleeve in the drawer.
