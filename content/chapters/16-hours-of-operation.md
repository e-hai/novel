# Hours of Operation

The Ward did not close the shop while it reviewed the shop. There was no mechanism for it. A licence could be suspended, and a suspension could be appealed, and an appeal could take a year, and during that year the shop stayed open, because a shop that is shut cannot be inspected.

So Elias worked. He worked Tuesday through Saturday, nine to six, and put a card in the window that said so, and the card was the third card he had put there in eleven years.

The first customer on Wednesday was a boy of about fourteen with a green coat too big for him and a school folder under his arm.

"I want one taken out," the boy said.

"Taken out of what?"

"Out of me."

Elias took an intake slip. "What is it?"

"A staircase." The boy looked at the counter. "There's a staircase at my grandmother's. I keep seeing it. It goes up to a door and the door's open and I can't—" He stopped. "I don't want to see it."

"You don't want to see it, or you don't want it to be the last thing?"

The boy looked up. It was a good question, and he was old enough to know it was a good question, and that made it worse for him.

"The last thing," he said.

Elias wrote on the slip: *Staircase. Door open. Wants removal.* Then he wrote, underneath: *Ask what is through the door.* He asked. The boy did not know. He had never gone up. He had stood at the bottom for two years, in a memory he had not been paid to forget, and looked.

"Go up the stairs," Elias said. "Then come back."

"That's it?"

"That's the repair. It's not one I can do in here."

The boy took the slip, folded it, and put it in the folder. At the door he stopped and said, "Do you charge for that?"

"No," Elias said. "That one's free. It's the same advice I give myself."

The second customer was a widow who wanted her husband's laugh taken out of a recording of a wedding.

"He laughs like he's apologising," she said. "Every time. It's the only thing left that's wrong."

Elias seated the core. The wedding was in a garden with folding chairs, and the husband was a heavy man in a light suit who laughed with his shoulders first. Elias listened to it four times. It was not a laugh that apologised. It was a laugh that had been interrupted and had decided to finish anyway.

"If I take it out," Elias said, "the rest fills in. He'll smile. It'll be smooth. You won't have a single thing wrong with him."

"That's what I want."

"That's what you asked for." He set his hand on the console. "I can't do it today. Come back Friday. If you still want it then, I'll do it."

She came back Friday and did not want it.

The third customer was a courier with a parcel of fill, and the parcel was the wrong size, and the courier wanted it signed for anyway. Elias signed, and then he opened it in front of the courier and found that the brick inside was stamped with a supplier's mark he did not recognise — not the Ward's mark, not the old Cinder mark, a third mark, a small circle with a line through it, like a lens with the shutter closed.

"Where's this from?"

"Warehouse," the courier said. "They all come from the warehouse now."

After the courier had gone, Elias took the rule sheet out of the drawer and laid it flat on the bench.

Three lines in his hand. Under them, in Mara's smaller, slanting hand, the line about the witness. He read the four lines the way he read a core: for what had been left out.

Then he took the pencil and wrote a fourth line, in his own hand, below hers:

*Two accounts, both kept. The core is not the record. The repair is.*

He looked at it. It was not a rule. It was a wish with a date under it. But he wrote the date anyway, and he put the sheet back in the drawer, and he shut the drawer with his hip, and he went to the window and turned the card around so that it faced the lane and said OPEN.
