# What the Ward Forgets

The hearing room was on the fourth floor and had windows, which was unusual for the Ward, and the windows looked out onto a car park and a wall.

There were three of them behind the table. One read the file, one took notes, and one looked at Elias the whole time as if he were a number that had come out wrong.

"Licence 4-119," the reader said. "Repair of 24 November. Unsealed. Second account entered by hand. A finding of a reading, filed by inspector Ader." She turned a page. "Mr Vane. Do you dispute the finding?"

"No."

"Do you dispute the method?"

"No."

"Then what are you here to say?"

He had prepared a dozen answers on the tram, and all of them had been about the seam. What he said was, "That the finding is the wrong shape."

The one who had been taking notes stopped writing. The one who had been staring went on staring.

"A repair is a closed thing," Elias said. "You put the memory back and you close it and you stamp it and it is correct. That's the trade. I've done it for eleven years. It works because nothing after the repair has to agree with anything before it."

"And the seam?"

"The seam is where the two accounts meet. The machine's account, which is what the loom did. And the client's account, which is what he could still say. They don't match. They can't match. A repair that closes is a repair where one of them is deleted."

The reader looked at the file. "The client's account is not evidence."

"The client's account is the client."

"That is a statement about your feelings."

"It is a statement about the record." He put his hand flat on the table. "You have a log that says what I did. You have a core that shows what he remembers. If they disagree, the file is not defective. The file is honest. You have been calling honesty a defect for forty years because a defect is cheaper to close."

The one taking notes wrote something. The one staring said, "This is not a review of the trade. This is a review of one repair."

"It's a review of the rule that made the repair unusual."

Mara stood up then. She had been sitting at the side, at the end of the table, with her hands folded, in the posture she used in hearings, which Elias had never seen before and recognised immediately.

"Licence 4-119 has a second signature on the rule sheet," she said. "Mine. I attended the repair. I entered the second account. If the board wants a witness who is not the reviewed party, I'm the witness. If the board wants a second licence holder who will accept the seam as a repair, then put my licence against his."

The reader turned to her. "You're 6-204."

"I am."

"Your licence was suspended in the matter that made you leave the trade."

"It was." Mara did not sit down. "It was suspended because I refused to close a repair. So you have a choice. You can suspend the man who left the seam, and the woman who refused to close it, and you can write in your file that the trade has no one left who will do either. Or you can write down what a seam is, and let it be a category, and let the next person who sees one know what to call it."

The room was quiet in the way rooms are quiet when three people are deciding separately.

The reader closed the file. "The board will consider a category. It will take time. In the meantime, licence 4-119 is not suspended. It is conditioned. All dual-account repairs must be attended by a second licence holder, named on the rule sheet before the repair, present at the loom."

"Named before," Mara said.

"Before." The reader looked at her. "You're volunteering."

"I'm being asked."

"Both are true."

Outside, in the corridor, the windows gave onto the car park, and the car park gave onto the wall. Elias stood at the glass until his breathing had come back to something he recognised.

"That was a good sentence," he said. "The one about honesty."

"It was a true one. It's easier to say true ones when you're not the one being reviewed." Mara put her coat on. "You'll need a new rule sheet. Five lines now."

"Yes."

"And you'll need to stop calling it a seam in front of the board."

"What should I call it?"

She thought about it all the way down the stairs. At the bottom, in the lobby, where the light was the colour of the light in the archive, she said, "Call it a joint. Joints are structural. Seams are what you mend. Nobody audits a joint."

He wrote it on the back of the intake slip from the hearing, under the two crossed-out words, and by the time he got back to Cinder Lane the pencil had worn a hole in the paper.
