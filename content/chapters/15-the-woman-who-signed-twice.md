# The Woman Who Signed Twice

The ninth floor was colder than the core had made it. That was the first thing Elias noticed, and it annoyed him, because he had spent a week telling himself that the cold in the engram was a construction.

It was not a construction. It was the air.

Mara had told him to come at four, when the archive staff changed over and the corridor belonged to nobody. She had also told him not to bring the core, and he had brought the core, in his coat, because he had never been good at being told.

The panels along the left wall lit as he passed, dimming behind him. Each one had a small brass plate with a number and no name. The right wall was the same, and the only difference between the two sides was that the right-hand plates were newer and had a second line on them, a line that showed who had reviewed the record and when.

At the end of the corridor, the blue door stood open. It had been open in the engram and it was open now, and Elias understood that it was always open, that the room beyond it was not a room you were taken into but a room that was simply never shut.

Inside, a woman in a grey coat was filing.

"Elias Vane," she said, without turning. "Licence 4-119. You're early."

"You knew I'd come early."

"Everyone comes early. The ones who don't come early don't come." She turned. Her lanyard had a number on it, and the number was the same number Elias had not been able to read in the core. "Ilse Renn. Depositions."

"The record says you were both."

"I was." She said it without defence, which was worse than a defence. "Sit down or stand. There's no chair that helps."

He stood. She went back to filing, and the files made a sound like cards being dealt.

"Depositor and reviewer," he said. "That's not a mistake."

"No."

"That's a choice."

"Yes." She closed the drawer and looked at him. "Do you know what happens to a record with one signature?"

"It gets reviewed."

"It gets closed. Reviewed means two people looked. One signature and a stamp is a closed file, and a closed file is a file nobody has to read again. I have worked this floor for nineteen years. I have watched four thousand records close. Do you know how many of them were read by anyone afterward?"

He did not answer.

"None," she said. "Not one. They close, and then they are correct, and then they are furniture."

She came out from behind the desk and walked past him to the panel at the end of the corridor, the one whose light was not amber but a duller, older colour, like a lamp left on in a room where someone has died.

"Your client's record had one signature," she said. "The son's. He deposited his mother's memory of him, and he signed, and the file would have closed in nine days. Then you repaired it in a way that left a seam, and the loom logged a second account, and the log made the file contested."

"So you signed it."

"I signed it," she agreed. "As depositor, because a contested file needs a living depositor to stay open. And as reviewer, because a contested file needs a second signature, and there was no second person on this floor who would put their name to yours."

He looked at the panel. The light under it was so old that it had a smell.

"You could have written to me," he said. "You could have told me a review was coming."

"I did tell you." She touched the panel with two fingers, the way people touch a door before opening it. "The letter is the telling. It is also the only way a review can be entered. The Ward does not accept warnings. It accepts letters."

"And the signature in my hand, in the core — the tenth, before the letter."

She was quiet long enough that the corridor's hum became the loudest thing in it.

"Records that have never been signed cannot be reviewed," she said at last. "So the archive signs them, in the hand of whoever is nearest in the log. It is not forgery. Forgery is when a person writes a name. This is when a system writes a reason."

"That's the same thing said slower."

"Yes," Ilse said. "It is."
