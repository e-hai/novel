# The Letter from the Ward

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the day the bread cart came, so Elias heard it before he saw it: the flap of the slot, then the flat slap of something too stiff to be a bill.

He had been at the bench with the awning brackets in his hand. Two of them, the left one bent where the wind had worked at it. He had promised himself he would put the sign back up before the rain came again. That had been a week of promises.

The envelope was the colour of a fingernail. The stamp was not a stamp but a wet-stamped seal, pressed slightly off-centre, so that the number on it sat crooked.

He knew the number. Everyone in the trade knew it. He had seen it once before, on a form he had signed at twenty-six, when he had still believed that a licence was a kind of permission rather than a kind of debt.

Mara was at the back, sorting sleeves. She had started coming in three mornings a week and had not yet asked for a key. She read the letter over his shoulder without asking whether she could, which was one of the things he had missed.

*Licence 4-119. Review of an unsealed repair performed 24 November. Attendance required at the archive on the ninth floor within ten days. Bring the log.*

"They'll want the log," she said.

"They always want the log."

"Then they'll have it." She took the paper from him and turned it to the light, looking for the watermark the way she used to look at cores. "They won't read it, though. They'll read the parts they already wrote."

He set the brackets down. Outside, the rain had stopped being weather and become a kind of grey attention. The lane was full of it. The bread cart's bell rang twice at the corner and then went quiet, as if it had decided not to come closer.

"Ten days," he said.

"Nine, if you count today as a day."

"I don't."

"Neither do they."

He went to the drawer for an intake slip and it caught again, the way it had since he put the second core in it. He lifted the front edge, cleared it, and did not look at what was inside. He had looked at it every day for a week and had learned nothing new. The sealed core sat in its paper sleeve. Beside it, the mother's core, face up, its label gone soft at the corner from being handled. Under both, the rule sheet, three lines in his hand and a smaller, slanting line in hers.

He took one slip and shut the drawer with his hip.

The letter he put under the drawer, where the light could not get at it. Then he stood in the middle of the shop for a while, listening to the loom cool. It ticked as it always did, unevenly, like a clock that had been repaired by someone who did not believe in clocks.

Mara came and stood beside him. "You could write back," she said. "You could tell them what you did. That the fill came from a witness. That there were two accounts and you kept both."

"I could."

"They'd have it in writing then."

"They'd have it in writing," he agreed.

She waited. When he said nothing more, she went back to the sleeves. He heard her sit, heard the paper move, heard her stop moving.

On the counter, a pencil lay where he had left it, beside the intake slips. He picked it up and wrote on the back of a slip, in the small cautious hand he used for labels:

*The repair was witnessed.*

He looked at the line for a long time. Then he crossed out *witnessed* and wrote, above it, *attended*. It was a worse word and a truer one. A witness could be produced in a room. Attendance could not.

He put the pencil down and stood looking at the two words, one above the other, the second one legible and the first one not.
