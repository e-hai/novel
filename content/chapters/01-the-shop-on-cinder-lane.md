# The Shop on Cinder Lane

The old man put two things on the counter: an engram with a chip in its rim, and a folded square of cloth.

Elias reached for the engram. The old man kept a finger on the cloth.

“Her laugh,” he said. “That's what I came for.”

Outside, rain ran down the metal awning. A seller at the end of Cinder Lane was wrapping the unsold bread, loosening each paper sleeve before tightening it again. People in the Ward saved everything that might still do one more job. The old man's coat had three different buttons. His thumb rested on the cloth as though it, too, needed holding together.

“What happened to the recording?” Elias asked.

“Nothing. Years.”

“And to her?”

The finger stopped moving.

“Years,” the old man said again.

Elias turned the core beneath the lamp. A cheap domestic model: six minutes of continuous storage, no separate sound track. He could already see the cloudy fringe where a part of the recollection had decayed. This would be filling, not restoration. He wrote both words on the intake slip, crossed out the first, and left the second where the client could read it.

The old man took out his coins.

“Not yet,” Elias said.

He carried the engram to the back of the shop and uncovered the loom. The old man followed, still holding the cloth. Elias usually discouraged that. Clients who watched the work tended to see the missing parts as things the machine had stolen.

This one sat on the stool without asking. His left knee did not bend properly.

The loom woke with a low vibration. It made the spoon in Elias's unwashed cup turn slightly against the rim.

The memory was a kitchen. Thin morning light came through a window whose lower pane had been repaired with opaque plastic. A woman stood at the counter, her back to the recorder. She wore a grey cardigan. Beside her was a white cup with a dark line along its handle.

Elias held the scene at the last intact frame. The woman had lifted her left hand. A breath entered the sound track, then the recording broke into a pale vacancy.

“She used to turn,” the old man said. “Just there. Then she'd laugh.”

“Do you remember why?”

“I don't need the why.”

Elias moved the scene back two seconds. The woman's left cuff had an irregular dark patch. He magnified it, looking for the stain's edge. The engram did not contain enough detail. The loom suggested a clean sleeve.

“Is that hers?” he asked, nodding at the cloth.

“Yes.”

“May I see it?”

The old man opened it on his knee. It was not a handkerchief, as Elias had thought, but a square cut out of something larger. One corner had been scorched. Two rows of stitches ran along the damaged side: neat grey ones and, over them, a wandering line of green.

“She kept it?”

“I did. After.”

There was no useful date, no name, nothing a scanner could resolve into a recoverable event. Elias left the cloth alone. He had a machine to operate, and the man had come to hear a laugh.

“The sound is gone,” he said. “I can make the movement continuous. I can't bring her voice back from an empty section.”

“You've seen people laugh.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

The old man looked past him to the suspended woman.

“Can you make it so I don't keep waiting for it?”

Elias had been asked that in many forms. He knew how to answer it with his hands.

He put in connective fill: a turning of the head, a small release of breath, a smile fitted to the available face. The synthetic tissue spread through the vacancy without resistance. At the cuff, the damage flag disappeared. The loom supplied an unmarked sleeve to match the other one.

He could have interrupted the operation there. The missing sound was the declared repair; the sleeve was a secondary correction, cheaper to leave in than to remove. He left it.

Forty minutes later the woman turned. Her shoulders moved naturally. Her smile was neither too broad nor too slow. Nothing in it belonged to a stranger, and nothing could be proved to belong to her.

The old man watched three times.

On the fourth he placed the cloth in his coat pocket and said, “There.”

Elias wanted him to say more. Instead, he stopped the playback.

At the counter, the old man counted the coins again. One was short. Elias lowered the price by that amount, an easier kindness than explaining the clean sleeve. On the receipt he wrote: **synthetic continuity; original interval unrecoverable.** He did not list every surface the machine had improved.

The man folded the receipt into the envelope with the engram.

“Will it stay?”

“The repair will.”

He had answered the question he was capable of answering.

After the client left, Elias rinsed his cup. Water darkened the line at the handle. He had glued it years ago; the repair held, but the crack stained a little more each winter. He dried it carefully and put it back beside the loom.

The shop had no sign. At one time it had had a licence, from a bureau that required repairmen to declare the exact proportion of synthetic content. Later the bureau had changed the requirement. It wanted a continuity score instead. His machine scored well. His customers seldom asked what the number meant.

A monthly inspector's form still arrived by post. Elias left it unopened under the till. He had stopped renewing the licence when the form ceased to offer a box for uncertainty.

At dusk he lifted the wet mat inside the doorway. An envelope lay beneath it, thin enough to have been pushed under the door. There was no return address. **E. Vane** had been printed on the front in a clerk's plain typeface.

Inside were a first-generation core and a blue paper token.

The core was older than the shop, older than the names the industry now used for its parts. Elias remembered seating these in wooden frames while the laboratory still smelled of varnish. This one had the shallow notch he used to make when an extraction had not been witnessed.

The token bore a retirement date: twenty years ago, to the day.

Along its bottom was a row of small numbered squares. The eighth square had been clipped out. Elias ran his finger over the missing place. His hand knew the movement, though he could not remember why anyone would retain a piece of punched paper for twenty years.

He turned the core over.

On the back, in his own handwriting, someone had written four words:

*Don't open this, Elias.*

He set it down at once. Then he picked it up again, cautiously, as if the instruction might be an account of something the core had already done.

The rain had stopped. Heat rose from Cinder Lane's corrugated roofs. He could hear the bread seller counting the last day's coins.

For twenty years Elias had followed two rules. Never promise to restore what was missing. Never work on his own memories.

The first had gradually become a sentence on his receipts.

He put the blue token beside the old man's intake slip. Both papers had an empty place, one clipped out and one crossed through. In the lamplight, the absence on each was perfectly legible.

He could not say the same for his rules.
