# The Shop on Cinder Lane

The shop had no sign. People found it the way they found everything in the Ward: by asking someone who knew someone, and then walking until the street narrowed and the rain smelled less like water and more like hot metal.

Elias Vane kept the loom in the back, under a sheet, because customers disliked watching the work. What they wanted was the envelope afterward, and the particular quiet that came over them when they played the engram back and found it whole.

The old man came in just after the afternoon shift, shaking a little.

"She laughed," he said. "I need her to laugh again. That's all."

Elias did not ask which she. He took the engram, a chipped thing no bigger than a coin, and seated it in the loom. The machine woke with a low sound, like a refrigerator remembering it used to be a motor.

Inside, the memory was a kitchen. Morning light. A woman at a counter with her back turned. The old man wanted her to turn around and laugh, but the part of the engram that held the laugh had gone grey years ago — rotted out, the way the cheap ones did. There was nothing to restore. There was only a hole shaped like a sound.

"You understand," Elias said, "I can't put back what isn't there."

The old man leaned closer. "Can you make it so I don't notice?"

"That I can do."

Elias worked for forty minutes. He keyed in synthetic connective tissue, fine as hair, bridging the gap where the laugh had been. It was honest fill — plausible, stable, keyed to the woman's face and the movement of her shoulders. When he played it back, the woman turned and smiled. The smile arrived clean and calm and carried no warmth at all.

The old man watched it three times. On the fourth he stopped the playback and said, "That's her."

"Is it?"

"She laughed like that." His voice was firm. He had already stopped hearing the difference.

Elias put the engram in an envelope and took the money. He did not tell the old man about the seam. Nobody ever thanked him for the seam. He had a rule about that too, though it was a smaller rule than the other one: never explain what you filled. Let them keep the shape of what happened. Let them call it back.

After the old man left, Elias covered the loom and went to the front of the shop. The rain had stopped. Cinder Lane steamed.

That was when he saw the envelope on the floor by the door.

It had been pushed under. No address, no name, just a single line in black ink: *for E. Vane.* The postage was paid in cash-stamps, the kind that couldn't be traced. Inside was an engram, and it was older than anything he had handled in years — a first-generation core, the sort they had made in the lab before the lab became a company.

He turned it over. On the back, in his own handwriting, were four words he did not remember writing:

*Don't open this, Elias.*

He stood in the doorway a long time, with the rain cooling on the metal around him, and considered the two rules he had made for himself. The first was about other people's memories. The second was about his own.

He had kept the first one perfectly.

He had no idea what had happened to the second.
