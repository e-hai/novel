# The Woman Who Kept Receipts

The woman came in out of the rain and set a folder on the counter, the way people set down something heavy they have been carrying too long.

"I want it back," she said.

Elias looked at the folder. It was an old engram case, the kind the clinics issued before the color changed to that reassuring blue. The corners were worn soft.

"Back where?"

"The feeling." She said it flatly, as if saying it any other way would cost her. "I had it done. Eleven years ago. The whole... the marriage. All of it. They said I'd be relieved. I was. For eleven years I was relieved." She touched the folder but did not open it. "Now I want it back."

He did not answer immediately. Outside, the rain kept working on the metal awning, that sound like water trying to boil.

"Do you know how repair works," he said, "or do you know what the clinics tell you?"

"I know what they told me. I know what I was promised. I want what they took."

He had heard versions of this. Not often — most clients came to forget, not to remember. But the shape was familiar: a person standing at the counter of a trade, asking it to reverse itself.

"Sit," he said.

She did not sit. She opened the folder and took out the engram, unlabeled, wrapped in a cloth that had been folded and refolded so many times it held the creases like a map.

"Eleven years," she said. "I can tell you the dates. I can tell you what he said, what I said, what we ate. All of it. It's all there." She tapped the cloth. "It's just... nothing. It's a report. I can read it and I know I was happy and I feel nothing when I read it. Do you understand? I have the facts. I don't have the—" She stopped.

Elias knew the word she would not say.

He put the engram on the loom and ran it. The memory was clean. Documentary. A kitchen, a man's face, a woman laughing at something — the movements all there, the timing perfect, the warmth gone out of it like air from a sealed room. He saw the seams at once, eleven years of them, smoothed so carefully that a lesser repairman would have called the work flawless.

It was flawless. That was the problem.

"It's a good repair," he said.

"I paid a lot for it."

"I believe you."

"Can you fix it?"

He looked at the seams. There was no technique for this. You could not inject feeling back into synthetic fill any more than you could warm a photograph by holding it. The original was not buried under the fill. The original was gone. What remained was the shape, and the shape was honest, and it was not enough.

"No," he said.

She took this without moving. "No."

"I can show you what happened. I can show you the seams. But I can't give you back what the fill replaced. Nobody can. What they took wasn't stored somewhere else. They overwrote it."

"Then what was I relieved of?"

"Pain," he said. "And the feeling with it. They come as one thing."

She was quiet for a while. Outside, a vehicle passed, tires hissing on wet stone.

"My daughter," she said suddenly. "She was three. She's grown now. She says, don't you remember? And I do remember. I remember everything she says I remember. But I don't—" She made a small sound, not quite a sob. "I don't feel it. I don't feel her."

Elias did not say the thing he was thinking, which was that this was the trade's oldest bargain and every client made it willingly. He had made it himself.

He took the engram off the loom and handed it back.

"I'm sorry," he said.

She wrapped it in the cloth again, carefully, the folds matching the old creases. At the door she paused.

"Will you take it," she said, "for the next one? The next person who comes in asking to have it done. Give them the envelope. Don't tell them what's in it. Just let them read the facts."

Elias looked at her.

"Would that be kind," she asked, "or cruel? I can't tell anymore."

He thought about his own drawer, and the rule written on the paper folded into it.

"I don't know," he said. "But I'll keep it."

She left. The rain took her. The awning kept its sound. Elias put the cloth-wrapped engram in the drawer beside his rule and closed it, and the shop was quiet except for the water, and the absence of anything else.
