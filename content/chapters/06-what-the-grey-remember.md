# What the Grey Remember

The family brought him in like a piece of furniture being returned.

"He was fine," the daughter said. "He knew all of it. He could tell you the year, the address, the name of every dog we ever had. He just—" She made a gesture at her chest. "He didn't care about any of it."

Elias looked at the man sitting between them. Old, clean, calm. A moderate grey — the family had clearly paid for the best available. Facts intact. Seams everywhere, smoothed to a uniform calm. The man was smiling with the expression of someone listening to a rehearsed story about a stranger.

"Where would you like me to start?" the man asked. Polite. Interested. Empty.

The daughter's mouth worked. "In there," she said, nodding at the loom.

Elias ran him. The memory was a model of the craft: a life fully documented. A wedding with the light correct, a child's birth with the faces correct, a house, a garden, a retirement party. Every seam invisible. Every fact present. Every feeling replaced with the clean calm of a well-made fill. There was nothing to repair because there was nothing broken — only a man who had been made unable to be hurt by his own life.

"Well?" the son said.

"There's nothing here to fix," Elias said.

"Fix the—" The daughter stopped. "Make him feel it again."

"You've had a grey before?"

"No." A pause. "We didn't know what it was called. We thought— they said he'd be himself."

"He is himself," Elias said. "The facts are his. The reactions are fill."

The man himself was listening with that same calm interest, as if this were a conversation about a third party.

"Do you remember your wife," Elias asked him, "and how you felt about her?"

"I remember her," the man said. "We were married forty-one years. She died in 2029. She liked—" He stopped, not because he had forgotten, but because the answer was a list and he had run out of list.

"Do you miss her?"

The man considered this the way one considers a question on a form. "I understand that I should," he said.

The daughter made a small sound and turned away.

Elias had given this news before. It never got easier and he did not want it to — that was the point. The ease was the danger. He could have smoothed this man further, removed the one remaining seam that held the knowledge of loss, and made him perfectly, finally calm. The family might have thanked him.

"They told us it was reversible," the son said.

"No," Elias said. "They told you what they needed you to hear."

The man smiled. "We give you back what happened," he said, in the flat, pleasant voice of a satisfied customer quoting an advertisement. He had no idea he was quoting anything.

Elias took him off the loom.

He did not charge them. The daughter left angry, which was better, he thought, than the calm they had brought in. The man followed them out, docile, polite, turning once at the door to say, "Thank you for your time," with perfect, unearned sincerity.

Elias sat in the quiet. Rain on metal. He thought about the woman with the receipts, and the boy with the blue door, and the serial number under his own loom, and his own drawer with the rule in it. All of them facts. All of them preserved. None of them, by itself, enough.

He wrote a line in the log he kept for no one: *The grey remember everything. That is the cruelty. Not the forgetting.*
